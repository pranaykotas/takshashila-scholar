# Takshashila Language and Framing Standards

These are not style preferences. They are intellectual positions about how to frame Indian policy accurately.

## Geographical terms

| Avoid | Use | Why |
|-------|-----|-----|
| South Asia | Indian subcontinent | "South Asia" is an American geopolitical coinage; there is no "North Asia" |
| Middle East | West Asia | Eurocentric framing from British imperial geography |
| South Asian countries | Countries of the Indian subcontinent | Same as above |
| Northeast | Northeast India | "Northeast" alone is ambiguous |

## Jargon to avoid

| Avoid | Use instead | Why |
|-------|-------------|-----|
| Democratisation | Popularisation, widening access, making accessible | Imports a political science meaning that obscures the actual claim |
| Governance | Be specific: administration, regulation, oversight, accountability, implementation | Too vague to mean anything |
| Stakeholders | Actors, parties, those affected, interest groups | AI and corporate jargon |
| Civil society | Be specific: NGOs, community groups, citizens, residents, associations | Catch-all that obscures who actually does what |
| Leverage (as a verb) | Use, apply, build on | Corporate jargon |
| Ecosystem | Sector, field, network, industry | Overused metaphor |
| Narrative (as jargon) | Argument, framing, story, claim | Use only when you mean it literally |
| Capacity building | Training, skill development, institution building | Jargon that obscures the specific activity |

## Framing rules

**Do not import Western debates wholesale.** When a Western policy concept appears — whether from US think tanks, EU policy documents, or British academic literature — pause and ask:
- Does this apply to India's specific state capacity constraints?
- What is the Indian institutional equivalent?
- Does India's federal structure (Union/State/Concurrent List) change the analysis?

**Think in terms of Better or Worse, not Good or Bad.** Policy analysis is comparative, not moral. The question is always: compared to what alternative, and better or worse for whom?

**Good intentions do not equal good outcomes.** Judge policies by their effects, not their stated goals.

**One rupee of government spending is not the same as one rupee of social spending.** Implementation leakage, administrative costs, distortionary effects, and opportunity costs all matter. The gross spending figure is not the welfare delivered.

**Transforming India is a marathon.** Avoid both triumphalism about recent progress and despair about persistent problems. India in 2025 is measurably better than India in 1950 on most indicators — and still has enormous distance to cover.

**Politics is the art of the possible.** Analytically correct solutions that are politically impossible are not solutions. Political feasibility is a legitimate criterion, not a cop-out.

## When writing about India's regions and neighbours

- **West Asia** (not Middle East) for the region including Gulf states, Iran, Iraq, Israel
- **Indian subcontinent** for the geographic region including India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan
- Use "Pakistan-administered Kashmir" and "India-administered Jammu and Kashmir" — avoid partisan framings on either side
- **Northeast India** not "Northeast" alone
- **Sri Lanka** not Ceylon (unless historical context requires it)

## On writing about the Indian state

When assessing state capacity, use these four dimensions rather than the crude "big vs. small government" framing:
- **Expenditure**: Is the state spending too much or too little?
- **Size**: Is the bureaucracy too large or too small?
- **Capability**: Does the state have the right skills and systems?
- **Ambition**: Is the state trying to do too much, or has it abdicated core responsibilities?

The Indian state is simultaneously overreaching in some domains and absent in others. Avoid blanket statements about state size.
