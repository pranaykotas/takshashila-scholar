# Paper Self-Review (Takshashila Standard)

Systematic pre-submission review for Takshashila policy outputs. Applies institutional writing standards, analytical quality checks, and editorial norms.

**Output types covered:** op-eds, policy briefs, discussion documents, working papers, conference papers.

---

## Step 1: Reverse Bollywood Format (RBF) — Lead with Conclusion

This is the single most important Takshashila writing standard. Check it first.

**The RBF rule:** The conclusion, recommendation, or key finding must appear in the first 60 seconds of reading — typically the first paragraph or the executive summary. Methodology, evidence, and supporting argument follow. Never bury the finding.

- Is the central recommendation or finding stated in the first paragraph?
- Can a reader who reads only the first 100 words know what the piece argues?
- Does the abstract/executive summary lead with the answer, or does it lead with the question?
- Are section headers outcome-oriented ("India should liberalise spectrum allocation") rather than process-oriented ("Analysis of spectrum policy options")?

**If RBF fails:** The draft must be restructured before anything else. Note the location of the buried finding and suggest where to move it.

---

## Step 2: Argument Quality

### Central claim
- Is the thesis stated in one sentence somewhere in the piece?
- Is the claim solution-oriented? (Takshashila standard: frame as "how to X" not "what are the challenges to X")
- Is the claim falsifiable — what would have to be true to disprove it?
- Is the claim bounded in time, geography, and domain?

### Causal chain
- Does the piece contain a mechanism, or just a correlation?
- Is every link in the causal chain independently sound?
- Are competing hypotheses acknowledged and addressed?

### Evidence
- Are empirical claims sourced and verifiable?
- Is evidence presented both for and against the recommendation? (Takshashila standard: literature review must include evidence on both sides)
- Is there selection bias — only evidence that supports the conclusion?
- Are sources appropriate for the Indian context? (Not just Western data generalized to India)

---

## Step 3: Indian Perspective Check

- Is the research question framed from an Indian perspective? ("How should India govern X" not "What does the literature say about X")
- Does the recommendation account for India's specific state capacity constraints?
- Are Indian institutional names correct? (DPIIT not "the trade ministry"; Union Budget not "the budget")
- Is the Concurrent/Union/State List relevant? If so, is federal complexity acknowledged?
- Does the piece import Western policy debates wholesale without checking Indian applicability?

---

## Step 4: 21st Century Realities

**Mandatory for:** conference papers, policy briefs, working papers, discussion documents.
**Optional for:** op-eds, blog posts.

Which of the four realities are materially relevant to this piece?

- **Climate Change** — Does the recommendation have climate implications (positive or negative)?
- **Energy Transition** — Does the analysis assume an energy mix that may shift within the policy's time horizon?
- **Information Age / AI** — Does the piece account for how digital networks and AI are changing the problem?
- **Complexity** — Does the piece assume linear causation where feedback effects and unintended consequences are likely?

For each relevant reality: does the piece address it, ignore it, or explicitly note it as outside scope?

---

## Step 5: Takshashila Voice and Register

- **Non-partisan framing** — Does the piece take policy positions, not partisan ones? Are consequences assessed by their effects on people, not by political alignment?
- **Pro-market, not pro-business** — If the piece recommends market solutions, are consumer interests and competition considered alongside firm interests?
- **Non-partisanship ≠ neutrality** — The piece can take a clear position; it should not avoid taking one to appear balanced
- **Flexible hypotheses** — Does the piece present evidence against its own argument? (A piece that ignores contradictory data is not Takshashila-standard)
- **No AI writing tells** — "It is important to note", "delve into", "nuanced", "multifaceted", "in conclusion", "navigate", "it is worth mentioning" — flag and remove
- **No jargon** — "governance", "stakeholders", "democratisation", "leverage" (as verb), "ecosystem", "capacity building" — replace with specific language

---

## Step 6: Structure and Format

For the output type being reviewed:

**Op-ed (600–900 words)**
- [ ] Hook in first sentence: specific fact, number, or event — not historical preamble
- [ ] One central argument; supporting points serve it
- [ ] Ends with concrete implication or recommendation, not a call for "more research"
- [ ] RBF: recommendation visible in first paragraph

**Policy Brief**
- [ ] Executive summary leads with problem and recommendation
- [ ] Evidence before options; options before recommendation
- [ ] Headers present; sections brief
- [ ] RBF: recommendation in executive summary, not page 4

**Discussion Document / Working Paper**
- [ ] Research question stated in introduction
- [ ] Hypothesis stated and tested
- [ ] Evidence for and against presented
- [ ] 21st century realities check done (mandatory)
- [ ] Conclusion matches introduction's promise

---

## Step 7: Citations and Sourcing

- Are all factual claims cited?
- Are parliamentary Q&A, government reports, and Indian institutional sources used where relevant?
- Are citations in Takshashila format?
- Are there claims that would make a reader say "source?" — flag these

## Quick Checklist

```
Pre-Submission Checklist:
- [ ] RBF: conclusion in first paragraph / executive summary
- [ ] Thesis stated in one sentence
- [ ] Claim is falsifiable
- [ ] Evidence on both sides presented
- [ ] Indian perspective: question and answer
- [ ] 21st century realities: checked (mandatory for briefs/papers)
- [ ] No AI writing tells
- [ ] No jargon without replacement
- [ ] Non-partisan framing
- [ ] Citations complete
- [ ] Format correct for output type
```
