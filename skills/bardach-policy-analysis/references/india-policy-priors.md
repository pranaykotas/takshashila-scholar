# India Policy Priors

These are mental models and priors that a Takshashila-trained policy analyst brings to any Indian policy problem. They are not rules — they are lenses that help identify where conventional analysis tends to go wrong in the Indian context.

---

## On the State and Policy

**Good intentions do not equal good outcomes.**
Judge policies by their effects on welfare, not their stated goals. India's policy history is full of well-intentioned programmes with poor outcomes — and a few poorly-intentioned reforms that produced significant welfare gains.

**Good policy with bad implementation is not good policy.**
Implementation is not a detail — it is the policy. Check the delivery pathway before endorsing a recommendation.

**One rupee of government spending is not one rupee of welfare.**
Leakage, administrative costs, distortionary effects, and opportunity costs all matter. The gross spending figure is not the welfare delivered. Ask: what is the effective incidence?

**Transforming India is a marathon.**
Avoid both triumphalism about recent progress and despair about persistent problems. India in 2025 is measurably better than India in 1950 on almost all indicators — and still has enormous distance to cover. Situate your analysis in this historical arc, not just the last five years.

**Politics is the art of the possible.**
Analytically correct solutions that are politically impossible are not solutions. Political feasibility is a legitimate criterion, not a cop-out. Name it, include it in your evaluation, and recommend accordingly.

**Capacity must change for policy to change.**
Recommending policies that require capabilities the state does not have is not analysis — it is wishful thinking. If your recommendation depends on state capability X, name what it would take to build X.

## On Markets and Regulation

**India's markets are not the textbook baseline.**
Thin markets, geographic fragmentation, weak contract enforcement, and information asymmetries are the norm, not exceptions. Do not assume markets would be efficient without the specific distortion you are analysing.

**Do not import Western regulatory debates wholesale.**
The US debate about deregulation was shaped by a specific state of infrastructure, contract enforcement, and market depth that India does not share. The EU's precautionary principle was developed in a specific political economy context. Ask: what is the Indian institutional equivalent? What does India's federal structure change?

**The informal economy is not a residual.**
India's informal sector is large, persistent, and interacts with formal institutions in complex ways. Policies that work through formal channels often have unintended effects in the informal sector. Check both.

## On Evidence and Analysis

**Better or worse, not good or bad.**
Policy analysis is comparative: compared to what alternative, and better or worse for whom? Every claim of "this policy is good" is implicitly comparative. Name the comparison.

**Not every unintended consequence is unanticipable.**
Force a second-order check on every policy option. What happens to adjacent systems? Who gains from this policy's failure? What does the causal loop diagram show beyond the first-order effect?

**Beware the McNamara Fallacy.**
What can be measured easily may not be what matters most. India has rich data on some indicators and near-zero data on others. Do not let data availability drive your choice of outcome metric.

## On Language and Framing

**Use Indian subcontinent, not South Asia.**
"South Asia" is an American geopolitical coinage from the Cold War era. If there is no North Asia, why is there a South Asia? Use "Indian subcontinent" for the geographic region.

**Use West Asia, not Middle East.**
"Middle East" is Eurocentric framing from British imperial geography. Takshashila uses West Asia consistently for the region including the Gulf states, Iran, Iraq, Israel, and the Levant.

**Prefer concrete over abstract.**
"The Railways Ministry" not "the transport governance apparatus." "MoEFCC" not "the relevant regulatory authority." Specificity is a virtue.

**Avoid jargon that obscures more than it reveals.**
- Democratisation → popularisation, widening access, making accessible
- Governance → administration, regulation, oversight, accountability (be specific)
- Stakeholders → actors, parties, those affected, interest groups
- Civil society → NGOs, community groups, citizens, residents (be specific)
- Ecosystem → sector, field, network, industry
- Capacity building → training, skill development, institution building
