# Bardach's 8 Steps — with India Annotations

Bardach's *A Practical Guide for Policy Analysis* (Eightfold Path) provides a systematic framework for policy analysis. These are the eight steps with India-specific guidance layered in at each stage.

---

## Step 1: Define the Problem

**What Bardach says:** State the problem precisely, not as an absence of your preferred solution. Frame it in terms of welfare loss, market failure, or coordination problem.

**India annotation:**
- Apply the four-dimension state capacity check: Is this a problem of government expenditure (too much/too little), size (bureaucracy), capability (skills and systems), or ambition (overreach or abdication)?
- Ask: Is this a Union List, State List, or Concurrent List issue? The federal structure often determines who can actually act.
- Avoid framing the problem as an absence of a policy that already exists elsewhere in the West — check whether the Indian institutional equivalent already exists or whether the context differs.
- Reference: `state-capacity-lens.md`

**Key question:** Compared to what alternative, and better or worse for whom?

---

## Step 2: Assemble the Evidence

**What Bardach says:** Gather projections, data, and evidence about the magnitude of the problem and the effects of potential solutions. Be selective and strategic — you cannot know everything.

**India annotation:**
- Primary sources for Indian policy: Ministry annual reports, CAG audit reports, Parliamentary Standing Committee reports, NITI Aayog policy papers, RBI reports.
- Use `/parliament-search` to find relevant committee reports.
- Use `government-source-finder` agent for ministry documents.
- India-specific baseline: India in 2025 is measurably better than India in 1950 on most indicators. Avoid both triumphalism and despair — situate data in the right historical baseline.
- Distinguish: What is known? What is estimated? What is assumed? Mark uncertainty explicitly.

**Key question:** Is this evidence specific to India's context, or imported from a different institutional setting?

---

## Step 3: Construct the Alternatives

**What Bardach says:** Identify all plausible policy alternatives, not just the "do nothing" vs. "ideal solution" dichotomy. Think creatively.

**India annotation:**
- All three sectors can fail: markets, governments, and societies. Do not default to either pure-market or pure-state solutions.
- Reference: `failure-modes.md` for the typology of market, government, and social failures.
- India-specific alternatives often include: regulatory approaches, public provision, PPP models, fiscal instruments (subsidies, taxes), capacity-building, information provision, or explicit non-intervention with stated reasons.
- Use `stakeholder-analysis` skill to check whose interests each alternative serves.

**Key question:** Have you included alternatives that challenge your prior? Have you included non-intervention as a genuine option?

---

## Step 4: Select the Criteria

**What Bardach says:** Define the criteria by which you will evaluate alternatives. Common criteria: efficiency, equity, political feasibility, administrative feasibility, legality.

**India annotation:**
- Think in terms of better or worse, not good or bad. Policy analysis is comparative, not moral.
- Political feasibility is a legitimate criterion, not a cop-out. Analytically correct solutions that are politically impossible are not solutions.
- Add: implementation feasibility — given existing state capacity, can this actually be delivered? Good policy with poor implementation is not good policy.
- Reference: `india-policy-priors.md` for common priors to check.

**Key question:** Are your criteria explicit and defensible, or are they smuggling in normative assumptions?

---

## Step 5: Project the Outcomes

**What Bardach says:** For each alternative, forecast outcomes on each criterion. This is inherently uncertain — acknowledge it.

**India annotation:**
- Not every unintended consequence is unforeseeable. Force a second-order check: what happens to adjacent systems when this intervention fires?
- Use `causal-loop-analysis` skill: render the causal map of projected outcomes using named loops. Identify reinforcing loops that may amplify the intervention (positively or negatively) and balancing loops that may dampen it.
- Ask: What is the time horizon? Short-run positive, long-run negative? Who bears the short-run cost?
- Distinguish: necessary conditions (without which the outcome is impossible) from sufficient conditions (alone enough to achieve it).

**Key question:** What does the causal loop diagram show? Which link in the causal chain is weakest?

---

## Step 6: Confront the Trade-offs

**What Bardach says:** Explicitly compare alternatives on all criteria. There is rarely a dominant option — acknowledge genuine trade-offs.

**India annotation:**
- Good intentions do not equal good outcomes. Evaluate by effects, not stated goals.
- One rupee of government spending is not one rupee of welfare. Implementation leakage, administrative costs, and opportunity costs matter — the gross spending figure is not the welfare delivered.
- If an alternative scores well on efficiency but poorly on equity (or vice versa), say so explicitly rather than papering over it.

**Key question:** Where do your criteria conflict? Who wins and who loses under each trade-off?

---

## Step 7: Decide

**What Bardach says:** Make a recommendation. Do not hide behind "further research is needed." The decision-maker needs a view.

**India annotation:**
- Capacity must change for policy to change. Before recommending, check: does the implementing agency have the capability, the mandate, and the resources?
- If recommending a state-capacity-dependent solution, name what capacity-building is required and how long it takes.
- Politics is the art of the possible. Your recommendation should be politically feasible, not just analytically optimal. If the first-best is politically impossible, recommend the second-best explicitly rather than pretending the first-best is achievable.
- Name the decision-maker your recommendation is addressed to: minister, regulator, Parliament, state government.

**Key question:** Who must act, and do they have the capability and mandate to act?

---

## Step 8: Tell Your Story

**What Bardach says:** Communicate your analysis effectively to the intended audience. The analysis is only as good as its communication.

**India annotation:**
- Narratives matter. Know whether your audience is a ministry official, a parliamentary committee, a newspaper reader, or a think-tank audience — the register differs.
- Apply Takshashila language rules: Indian subcontinent not South Asia; West Asia not Middle East; avoid imported Western debate framings.
- Indian English, plain language, domain-expert confidence — not hedged bureaucratic prose.
- Reference: `rules/takshashila-language.md` for vocabulary standards.
- Use `op-ed-writing` or `policy-brief-writing` skill for the final output.

**Key question:** What does your audience already believe, and what does your analysis add?
