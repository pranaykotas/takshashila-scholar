# Failure Modes: Markets, Governments, and Societies

All three sectors can fail. Policy analysis must avoid defaulting to either pure-market or pure-state solutions. The question is always: which sector is better placed to address this specific failure, and what are the second-order failure risks of the intervention?

---

## Market Failures

Markets fail when they produce inefficient or inequitable outcomes without intervention.

| Type | Description | Indian Examples |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| Public goods | Non-excludable, non-rival — markets underprovide | National defence, basic research, street lighting |
| Externalities | Private costs/benefits diverge from social costs/benefits | Air pollution from industry, vaccine herd immunity |
| Information asymmetry | One party knows more than the other | Health insurance, used goods, financial products |
| Natural monopoly | Average costs decline at all output levels — one firm serves most efficiently | Railways, electricity transmission, water supply |
| Coordination failure | Each actor waits for others to move first | Industrial clusters, technology standards, vaccination campaigns |

**India-specific note:** Market failures in India are often compounded by thin markets (few buyers/sellers), geographic fragmentation, and weak contract enforcement. Do not assume that markets would work efficiently absent government intervention — India's market infrastructure is still developing.

---

## Government Failures

Government interventions can themselves produce inefficient or inequitable outcomes.

| Type | Description | Indian Examples |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| Information failure | Government lacks the information that market prices convey | Central planning, input-output ratios, production targets |
| Incentive failure | Government actors maximise their own interests, not public welfare | Regulatory capture, rent-seeking, bureaucratic self-preservation |
| Implementation failure | Gap between policy design and policy delivery | MGNREGS leakage, direct benefit transfer errors, last-mile delivery |
| Political failure | Policies are designed to win elections, not to maximise welfare | Freebies, farm loan waivers timed before elections |
| Regulatory overreach | Regulation creates compliance costs that exceed the problem's harm | Licence raj, environmental clearance delays |

**India-specific note:** Good policy with poor implementation is not good policy. India has a significant stock of well-designed policies that fail at the implementation stage. Always check the implementation pathway before recommending.

---

## Social/Community Failures

Markets and governments are not the only actors. Communities, families, and social norms also allocate resources — and also fail.

| Type | Description | Indian Examples |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| Exclusion | Community norms exclude particular groups from resources | Caste discrimination, gender exclusion from land ownership |
| Collective action | Community cannot coordinate without external enforcement | Groundwater depletion, common-pool resource mismanagement |
| Norm persistence | Harmful norms persist even when members would prefer to change | Dowry, early marriage, open defecation |
| Capture | Local institutions are captured by dominant groups | Gram panchayat capture, local contractor networks |

---

## Using the Typology in Bardach Step 3

When constructing alternatives:
1. Identify the primary failure type.
2. Match the intervention type to the failure — market failures often call for regulation or public provision; government failures may call for decentralisation or competition; social failures may require information campaigns or legal reform.
3. Check: does your preferred intervention introduce a new failure of a different type? (e.g., regulation to fix a market failure may introduce regulatory capture)
4. Always include non-intervention as a genuine option with its costs stated explicitly.
